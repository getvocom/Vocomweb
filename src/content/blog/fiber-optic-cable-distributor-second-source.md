section1Title: "Allocation is a real thing and it does not favor you"
section1Body: "Corning reported its third quarter on 27 October. Optical Communications revenue was up 44 percent year over year, with enterprise network sales inside that up 84 percent. On the optics side, Lumentum's management has described its order book as effectively sold out into 2029. Capacity across this industry is being contracted years ahead by very large buyers.\n\nWhich raises a question that does not get asked out loud very often. When a manufacturer is running against commitments like those and a new order arrives, who gets served?\n\nThe industry has been fairly candid about the answer. Speaking to Fierce Network in January, a partner manager at cable manufacturer Superior Essex put it plainly: vendors are \"just going to cater to the ten million versus the ten thousand dollar deal.\" In the same piece she noted that data center builds had been able to leapfrog federally funded broadband projects, because data centers were buying capacity every day.\n\nNone of that is scandalous. It is how any constrained market works. But it has a specific consequence for anyone who sells cable rather than makes it, and that consequence is the subject of this piece."
section2Title: "What allocation actually does to a distributor"
section2Body: "A distributor's business rests on being the person who can get it. Not the cheapest, not the biggest, the one who answers yes.\n\nIn a loose market that promise is easy to keep, because stock covers the common requests and a manufacturer will take a modest order without much fuss. In an allocated market it starts to fail in a particular pattern.\n\nThe common items stay fine. Standard counts, standard constructions, the things that move fast enough to justify holding. Those are not where the problem shows up.\n\nIt shows up on the awkward line. A jacket rating the inspector wants and nobody carries. A count that sits between two shelf items. A pre-terminated drop cut to a length. A polarity method that has to match something already installed. Historically those got sourced with a phone call. Under allocation they get quoted at a date that does not work, or they get declined, because the manufacturer is prioritizing its large contracts and a one-off special is the easiest thing to push down the queue.\n\nThe damage is not the lost line. It is what follows. A customer who hears no on the awkward item goes and finds somebody who says yes, and once that relationship exists they tend to move the standard business across too, because consolidating suppliers is easier than managing two. The special order was never the revenue. It was the thing protecting the revenue."
section3Title: "What a second source is, and what it is not"
section3Body: "A second source is not a backup distributor. Buying the same shelf items from another holder of the same stock does nothing in an allocated market, because both of you are drawing on the same constrained pool and both of you will be told the same thing.\n\nA second source worth having is structurally different from the first. The useful shape is a build-to-order supply route: production booked against your specification rather than drawn from somebody's warehouse, with its own manufacturing relationships and its own place in the queue.\n\nThat changes three things. It means the awkward line becomes quotable, because build-to-order has no concept of an item not being stocked. It means the inventory risk stays off your balance sheet, since nothing is bought until the customer order exists. And it means the lead time is a production schedule you can see rather than an allocation decision happening somewhere you cannot.\n\nWhat it does not do is beat stock on speed for standard items. If a customer needs a common count tomorrow and you have it on a shelf, the shelf wins and it always will. A second source is for everything the shelf cannot answer, which in this market is a growing share of what gets asked."
section4Title: "Running it without it becoming a second job"
section4Body: "The practical objection is overhead. Nobody wants to manage another supplier relationship for occasional orders.\n\nThe way it works is to treat it as a quoting capability rather than a supply line. The customer sends a requirement. You price it as your own, because it is your account and your relationship. The specification goes out once, properly, with every field finished: count, construction, fiber grade, jacket rating, tensile, termination, length and required date. What comes back is a price and a production date you can put in front of the customer without caveats.\n\nThe mistakes are consistent. Treating the second source as a last resort and only going to it once the first has already said no, by which time the schedule has eaten the slack. Sending a partial specification and then losing a week in emails. Quoting the customer a date before you have one. And the most expensive one, quietly declining the awkward line rather than pricing it, because declining looks free and it is not.\n\nTwo things are worth asking any build-to-order source before you rely on it. What is the honest lead time for this exact specification today, not the catalog figure. And what happens to that date if the order doubles.\n\nIt is also worth agreeing the shipping terms before the first order rather than during it. Build-to-order usually ships from the production side, so whether the price is FOB origin or delivered to a named destination changes both the landed cost and who carries the risk if a port or a customs line slips. That is a five minute conversation at the start of a relationship and a fortnight of argument in the middle of one.\n\nThe last piece is internal. A quoting capability only works if the people taking customer calls know it exists and know what it covers. The common failure is not the supply route, it is a salesperson who has an awkward requirement in front of them, does not think of it, and declines out of habit. Whatever form that takes in your business, a line in the quoting process or a note on the product list, it needs to be somewhere the person on the phone will see it at the moment they need it.\n\nDone properly the whole thing is invisible to the customer. They asked for something unusual, you priced it with a date, it arrived. They do not need to know where it came from, and the next time they have a standard order they do not go looking elsewhere."
