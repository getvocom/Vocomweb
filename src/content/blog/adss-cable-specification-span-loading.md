section1Title: "The question behind every ADSS enquiry"
section1Body: "Most ADSS enquiries arrive in the same shape. A fiber count, a span, and a loading district. Something like 432 fiber, 200 foot average span with a 400 foot maximum, NESC light.\n\nThree numbers, and between them they decide almost everything about the cable you can actually hang. What they do not do is decide it independently, which is where most specifications go wrong. Push any one of them and the other two move.\n\nThis is a guide to how they interact, written for people putting a spec together rather than for people who already build this for a living.\n\nStart with what the cable is being asked to do. All-dielectric self-supporting cable carries its own weight across the span. There is no messenger wire, no lashing, and no metallic element anywhere in the construction.\n\nThat last point is the whole reason it exists. Because nothing in the cable conducts, it does not need bonding or grounding, and it can be installed in or near the supply space on a power structure without creating a path to ground. On a joint-use pole line that is the difference between a straightforward install and a long conversation with the utility.\n\nThe tradeoff is that the cable itself has to be strong enough. On lashed cable the messenger takes the load. On ADSS the aramid yarn inside the jacket takes it, and everything in the specification follows from how much load there is going to be."
section2Title: "Loading district is the number people skip"
section2Body: "NESC divides the country into three loading districts, and the district decides what the cable is assumed to be carrying on its worst day.\n\nLight loading assumes no radial ice, high wind and a moderate temperature. Medium assumes a quarter inch of radial ice with lower wind and a colder temperature. Heavy assumes half an inch of ice. A cable that is comfortable at a given span under light loading can be well outside its working tension at the same span under heavy, because ice does two things at once: it adds weight, and it increases the diameter presented to the wind.\n\nThis matters more than people expect for one specific reason. Ice loads scale with the surface area of the cable, so a larger diameter cable in a heavy district is penalized twice over. It is heavier to begin with and it collects more ice.\n\nIf you are specifying across a route that crosses districts, specify to the worst one on the route. Splitting a build into two cable types to save money on the lighter section almost never pays for the splice cases and the inventory split it creates."
section3Title: "Span and fiber count pull against each other"
section3Body: "Here is the part that catches people out.\n\nHigher fiber counts mean a larger cable. A larger cable is heavier and presents more area to wind and ice. More load at the same sag means more tension, and tension is the thing the cable's rated strength has to cover. So as fiber count goes up, the span you can hold at an acceptable sag comes down, unless you accept more sag or specify a stronger construction.\n\nAt the lighter end of the range, 432 fiber at 200 to 400 foot spans under light loading, none of this is difficult. That is ordinary distribution line geometry and the cable is not working hard.\n\nThe arithmetic gets real somewhere past 600 to 800 foot spans, or when high counts meet heavy loading, or on long crossings where the sag has to stay inside a clearance envelope. At that point the specification stops being a catalog lookup and becomes a sag and tension calculation for the actual route, with the actual structure heights and the actual clearances.\n\nThe practical rule: give your supplier the span distribution rather than the average. An 820 mile build with a 200 foot average and a 400 foot maximum is a completely different cable problem from one with a 200 foot average and a 900 foot maximum crossing, and the average hides it.\n\nSag is the other half of this and it is worth being explicit about, because it is the variable most often left to the supplier by default. Sag and tension trade directly against each other. Allow more sag and the tension in the cable drops, which means a lighter construction will do the job. Allow less and tension climbs.\n\nThe limit on sag is not the cable. It is clearance. Ground clearance, clearance to the conductors above, clearance at the lowest point of a span over a road or a rail crossing. Those are set by code and by the structures you are hanging from, and they are fixed long before anyone picks a cable.\n\nSo the sequence runs one way only. Structures and clearances set the sag you are allowed. The sag you are allowed, together with the loading district, sets the working tension. The tension sets the construction. Starting at the cable and working backwards is how a build ends up with something that is fine on paper and short on clearance at the one crossing that matters."
section4Title: "The jacket decision, and the one that gets missed"
section4Body: "Two specification points sit outside the span arithmetic and both are worth getting right at order time.\n\nThe first is the jacket. Standard polyethylene is fine on most distribution structures. On higher voltage transmission structures, the electric field around the cable can drive dry-band arcing on a wet, contaminated jacket surface, and over time that damages standard PE. Where space potential is high enough, the specification calls for a track-resistant jacket instead. This is not an upgrade to take on general principle, because it costs more and is unnecessary on most pole lines, but it is expensive to discover after installation.\n\nThe second is fiber type and tube construction, which nobody asks about and everybody assumes. Loose tube and central tube ribbon behave differently at splice points and in the splice case, and the choice affects crew time across 820 miles far more than it affects cable price.\n\nBoth of these are faster to settle in a sample and a spec sheet than in a procurement thread. A sample costs a supplier very little and it answers questions that no datasheet does, including how the jacket prints, how the tubes behave when a crew opens them cold, and whether the construction matches what the drawing said it would be. On a build measured in hundreds of miles, an afternoon spent on a sample is the cheapest risk reduction available."
